# Design principles
Source: https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/design-principles ·
Section: Getting started · Ingested: 2026-09-28 · Apple change log: **June 8, 2026 —
design principles reintroduced** (WWDC26; companion talk "Principles of great design",
WWDC26 session 250 — "a deep dive into fundamental design principles for Apple platforms").

## In one line
Eight principles — **Purpose, Agency, Responsibility, Familiarity, Flexibility, Simplicity,
Craft, Delight** — are the foundation under every other HIG page. They are not a recipe; they
are the lens for weighing trade-offs when priorities compete.

## Framing (what Apple says about the principles themselves)
- Great, long-lasting design comes from understanding **how people think, feel and act** in
  the world. The principles encode that understanding.
- All other HIG guidance is built on top of these eight.
- There is **no single correct way** to apply them. Use them as decision tools: when two good
  goals conflict, ask which principle each serves and what the person needs most.

## The eight principles — short form (Apple's own tagline, our wording of the gist)
| # | Principle | Tagline (essence) | Symbol on the page |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Purpose** | Make something meaningful — start from intent; find what matters most to your people and make *that* excellent. | three concentric circles (a target) |
| 2 | **Agency** | Let people do things their own way — the UI serves their goals: freedom to act, awareness of what's happening, easy recovery. | person with arms and legs spread (the accessibility figure) |
| 3 | **Responsibility** | Act in people's best interest — your product affects lives; earn trust through safety, privacy and honesty about what it does and why. | two people clasping hands |
| 4 | **Familiarity** | Build on what people know — lean on concepts they already understand, rooted in established physical and digital patterns, applied consistently. | house |
| 5 | **Flexibility** | Adapt to diverse contexts and needs — people use software in unique ways; support as many devices, interaction types and perspectives as you can so everyone feels welcome. | two crossed double-headed arrows |
| 6 | **Simplicity** | Be clear and direct — remove the unnecessary; every element must earn its place; logical organisation and easy navigation. | scissors |
| 7 | **Craft** | Care about every detail — the design shows how much you care; take the time to do it well. | drawing compass |
| 8 | **Delight** | Make it human — people remember how a product made them feel; choose the right emotions and deliver them in a satisfying, enriching, joyful way. | heart |

## Rules (full detail, grouped exactly as the page groups them)

### 1. Purpose
- **should — Create value.** Keep the whole effort oriented to what makes the product
  *genuinely useful*. At every stage, ask what the product is for and whether each design
  decision serves that.
- **should — Keep focused.** Rank features by how people actually want to use the app; pour
  effort into making the most important ones truly great. A product with a clear use helps
  people reach their goals better than one that does many things vaguely.
- **should — Find new ways to solve the problem.** Study existing solutions and don't simply
  rebuild them. Define what makes your product different and let the design express that
  difference.

### 2. Agency
- **should — Stay out of the way.** People come to get things done; the best help is usually
  taking them straight to the task or content. Great design is unobtrusive yet there the
  moment it's needed.
- **should — Give people the freedom to explore.** Let people move around and reach features
  without being trapped in fixed flows or modes. If a guided flow is truly needed, make it
  easy to **skip or exit** so people reach the main experience fast.
- **should — Help people recover from mistakes.** When people know actions can be reversed or
  they can go back, they explore more freely — and that makes the interface more inviting.
  Build in forgiveness and make it effortless; recovering from the unexpected must not cost
  people their time or their work.

### 3. Responsibility
- **must — Be fully transparent about what the product does and why.** The relationship
  starts at the first interaction; make the app's intentions clear from the start. Give a clear
  reason whenever you ask for a permission. When collecting data, be explicit about *what* you
  collect and *how* it's used.
- **must — Keep people's information safe.** People trust you with their data's integrity.
  Collect only what the product needs to work, handle it carefully, think ahead about how it
  could be misused or cause harm, and build protections against abuse and unintended
  consequences.

### 4. Familiarity
- **should — Use concepts people know.** People arrive with knowledge from the physical world
  and from other software; draw on both so the interface feels familiar and intuitive.
- **must — Keep visuals and interactions consistent.** Once an element has a behaviour or look,
  use it the same way everywhere. Consistency speeds learning and gives confidence that new
  interactions will behave as expected.
- **must — Provide clear feedback.** Always signal what's happening: show when controls are
  available, indicate when content changes, and use **system patterns** for alerts and
  choices. Consistent feedback keeps people informed and in control.

### 5. Flexibility
- **must — Design for everyone.** Products designed with people in mind empower them. Consider
  the full diversity of people who might meet your design — their experiences, perspectives
  and needs. **Accessibility is a priority from day one**, not a late addition. Inclusive
  design reaches the widest audience and improves the experience for all.
- **should — Preserve a person's context.** As the design adapts across platforms and
  configurations, people should still feel at home: keep content and controls in consistent,
  predictable places and use **natural animations** to smooth transitions.
- **should — Consider a variety of input methods.** Support as many inputs as possible —
  voice, touch, keyboard and more — so more people can use the product the way that suits them.
- **should — Approach every platform with intention.** The software should feel polished and
  native wherever it runs; give every supported platform the **same level of care** (no
  second-class ports).

### 6. Simplicity
- **must — Include just what's necessary.** *Simplicity is not minimalism.* Aim for a focused,
  useful experience that keeps important things within reach and lets the rest fall away.
  (Removing useful things to look minimal violates this principle.)
- **should — Be concise.** The simplest phrasing is usually the most universal and helpful.
  Use exactly the words needed to explain a concept or label a control — no more.
- **should — Establish hierarchy.** When form and function are obvious, people know how to get
  the result they want. Favour **recognisable controls** and a **consistent structure** that
  tells people where they are and what comes next.

### 7. Craft
- **should — Quality sets the tone.** Every element signals how much you care. Make each
  decision deliberately; aim for stunning visuals, smooth animation, precise wording and
  thoughtful audio — all four are part of craft.
- **should — Experiment and iterate.** Prototype early, try new approaches, be willing to throw
  away what doesn't work. Hold each feature to a high bar, refine, try again. Test in
  real-world conditions to prove it is durable, reliable and fast.
- **should — Maintain your craft.** Shipping is not the finish line. Keep the interface current
  with new platform capabilities and design patterns and keep the quality bar high — design is
  an ongoing commitment.

### 8. Delight
- **should — Identify the emotion you want to inspire.** Software shouldn't all feel the same:
  fitness might energise, meditation might calm, a game might thrill. Decide the feeling first
  and let it shape the design.
- **should — Create defining moments.** Every interaction — from a button press to an error
  message — can express what the product stands for. Look for chances to add a touch of
  character that reflects the product's spirit.
- **must — Don't mistake delight for decoration.** People are trying to finish a task; delight
  pursued for its own sake must never obstruct the core purpose. Pick the overall aesthetic
  deliberately: some products want a considered practical touch, others some whimsy —
  experiment to find the balance.
- **Consider the whole.** Delight is the *sum* of all the care in the product — the freedom to
  act, the safety to explore, the comfort of familiar metaphors, the flexibility to move between
  contexts. Designing with intent, focus and care produces a product people find naturally
  delightful. (i.e. delight is an outcome of principles 1–7, not a layer added on top.)

## Web translation — how each principle becomes concrete UI decisions
| Principle | Concrete web/app decisions (and where our field notes already do it) |
|---|---|
| Purpose | Before drawing, write the screen's one job in a sentence. Cut or demote features that don't serve it. "One surface, one idea" (principles §11). |
| Agency | Deep-link people straight to the task; no forced tours. Every wizard has visible **Back** and **Close** (components § Wizard shell). Destructive actions get undo or confirmation; autosave drafts; never lose form input on error or navigation. Show progress/status for async work. |
| Responsibility | Explain *why* before asking for a permission (consent screen: say what each scope does, allow per-scope choice, "you can remove this later"). Collect minimal data; label what is stored. No dark patterns (pre-checked marketing boxes, hidden cancel). |
| Familiarity | Use native-feeling controls (iOS-style list rows, circle selection, segmented control, switches) rather than novel widgets. One button grammar, one row grammar per product. Use system-style alerts/sheets for choices. Hover/pressed/disabled/focus states on every control. |
| Flexibility | Accessibility from day one: semantic HTML, labels, `aria-*`, focus-visible rings, 44px targets, contrast ≥ 4.5:1 body, `prefers-reduced-motion`, `prefers-color-scheme`, zoom to 200% without breaking, RTL-safe layout (logical properties). Keyboard + touch + pointer + screen reader all work. Keep controls in the same place across breakpoints; animate layout changes gently. Mobile web gets the same care as desktop. |
| Simplicity | Simplicity ≠ minimalism: don't hide important actions to look clean. Concise labels ("Save", not "Click here to save your changes"). Clear hierarchy: one primary action, recognisable controls, a title that says where you are. |
| Craft | Pixel-level consistency (shared tokens module), smooth 150–300ms transitions, precise microcopy, test on real devices/slow networks, render every state (empty, loading, error, long text). Revisit screens as the platform evolves. |
| Delight | Decide the product's emotion (e.g. calm and trustworthy for a business tool). Put character into *moments* — empty states, success screens, error messages — never into decoration that slows the task. Our "minimal ending" (giant "You're ready" + one button) is a defining moment done with restraint. |

## Checklist
- [ ] Can you state the screen's purpose in one sentence, and does every element serve it?
- [ ] Can people skip/escape any guided flow and undo mistakes without losing work?
- [ ] Every permission/data request explains why, and only necessary data is collected?
- [ ] Controls, patterns and feedback are familiar and consistent across the product?
- [ ] Accessible from the start (keyboard, screen reader, contrast, motion, text size), all
      inputs supported, every platform/breakpoint equally polished?
- [ ] Nothing unnecessary — but nothing important hidden for the sake of minimalism?
- [ ] Wording concise; hierarchy obvious (where am I, what's next)?
- [ ] Details crafted: visuals, motion, words (and sound if any); tested in real conditions?
- [ ] The intended emotion is defined, and delight never gets in the way of the task?
